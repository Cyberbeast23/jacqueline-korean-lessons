Baekgu Comes Home

Based on a famous true story from South Korea.

Off the southwest tip of Korea lies Jindo Island, a place of green hills, rice fields, and a very special kind of dog. The Jindo dog is medium-sized, with pointed ears, a curled tail, and a thick coat that is often pure white. Koreans love these dogs for their courage and, above all, for being loyal. There is an old saying on the island: "Once your master, always your master."

In the spring of 1993, a white Jindo dog named Baekgu (백구, "white dog") lived with an elderly grandmother in a small village on the island. Baekgu had been born in that house. She knew every path to the fields and every stone wall in the village. But the family needed money, and in March they made a hard decision. Baekgu was sold to a new owner in Daejeon, a busy city about 300 kilometres to the north.

Daejeon was nothing like Jindo. Instead of sea wind and fields, there were car horns, concrete, and crowds of strangers. Baekgu would not settle. One day, she slipped away from her new home and vanished. Her new owner searched the streets, but she was gone.

No one knows exactly which way Baekgu travelled. Three hundred kilometres is a long way for a dog: there were mountains to climb, rivers to cross, busy roads to dodge, and towns full of unfamiliar smells. She had no map and no one to guide her. Scientists think dogs like Baekgu may use a powerful homing instinct, helped by their amazing sense of smell, to sense the direction of home. Whatever guided her, she kept going, week after week, through the summer heat and the first cool winds of autumn.

Seven months later, on a night in October, the grandmother heard a faint scratching at her gate. When she opened it, she could hardly believe her eyes. There stood Baekgu, but she looked terrible. Her white fur was matted and grey with dirt, her ribs showed through her skin, and her paws were sore. She was haggard and exhausted, yet her tail was wagging.

The grandmother wrapped her arms around her dog and cried. It was a tearful reunion. The family decided right then that Baekgu would stay with them for good. This time, they promised, they would never let her go.

The story spread across Korea. Newspapers and TV stations told it, and it later became cartoons, a documentary, and a children's book. Baekgu lived happily with her family for about seven more years. Today, a statue of a white dog stands in her village, which people now call the "Village of the Dog Who Came Back."

When visitors stand in front of the statue, many of them ask the same question: what would make an animal walk 300 kilometres? The villagers have a simple answer. Baekgu wasn't looking for a place. She was looking for her family.

Part 2: Think Deeper (not scored)

In 2 to 4 sentences, explain what the villagers mean when they say, "Baekgu wasn't looking for a place. She was looking for her family." Use at least two details from the passage as evidence (for example: Daejeon felt strange, the 300 km journey, the wagging tail, the tearful reunion).

Model answer: The villagers mean that Baekgu didn't return because she missed Jindo Island itself but because she loved the grandmother and her family. In Daejeon she had a new home, yet she still would not settle and ran away. She then struggled for seven months across mountains and rivers, and even though she arrived haggard and starving, her tail was wagging when she saw the grandmother. Her loyalty to her family, not to a place, is what kept her going.
