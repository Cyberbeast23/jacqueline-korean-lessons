✍️ Writing Day 8: Problem-Solution (The Rushed Morning)

Jacqueline, it's problem-solution week again (last time you rescued an autumn field trip from a rainy forecast). This week's problem lives on the clock: rushed, late mornings, just like Mina's in the reading. Name the problem, explain its cause and effect, propose realistic solutions with reasons, answer an objection, and finish strong.

Part 1: The Lesson

Problem → Cause / effect → Solutions → Best choice → Conclusion

You already know how to state a problem, give solutions with reasons, and answer an objection with however. Level up this week: compare your solutions and choose the best one. After you offer two fixes, add a sentence that weighs them ("Of these two ideas, the most effective is… because…"). Strong writers don't just list ideas; they judge them.

1. Problem: state it in one clear topic sentence
2. Cause / effect: why it happens and who it hurts (use a real detail, a time or a number)
3. Solution 1: a specific fix + reason
4. Solution 2: another fix + example, and answer an objection
5. Best choice + conclusion: which fix works best and why it's worth trying

Useful linking words: because, since, as a result, one solution is, another option, for example, some people may argue, however, of these ideas, the most effective, therefore.

Model Problem-Solution Paragraph

Problem (topic sentence)Cause / effectSolutions + reasonsBest choice + conclusion

Many students begin the school day rushed and late because their mornings are too crowded. When the alarm rings at 6:50 but a student snoozes until 7:15, twenty-five minutes of jobs must be squeezed into ten, and the hunt for a lost shoe or a library book can easily mean missing the 7:58 train; as a result, the student arrives stressed and misses the start of class. One solution is to move jobs to the night before, such as packing the bag and laying out clothes, because every job finished at night is one less job in the morning rush. Another option is to place the alarm across the room so that the student must get up to switch it off. Some people may argue that this is annoying; however, a few seconds of grumbling are better than twenty minutes of panic. Of these two ideas, preparing the night before is the most effective, because it removes the biggest time thief: searching. With a little planning, the morning race can become an easy walk to the station.

Notice: The cause-and-effect sentence uses real times (6:50, 7:15, 7:58), each solution has a reason, the writer answers an objection with however, and the conclusion chooses the best solution and explains why.
