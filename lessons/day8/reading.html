Mina Races the Clock

In Seoul, the city wakes up before the sun. By six o'clock, delivery scooters are buzzing down the alleys, shop owners are rolling up their metal shutters, and the first subway trains are rumbling under the streets. Mina's alarm rings at 6:50. On a good day, she is out the door by 7:45. Lately, there had not been many good days.

Mina's mornings had a pattern. She would turn off the alarm, close her eyes "for one minute," and wake up again at 7:15. Then the scramble began: hunting for a missing sock, packing her bag, gulping down a few bites of rice and soup while her grandmother shook her head. She would dawdle in front of the mirror, then sprint to the station. Three times in two weeks, she watched the doors of the 7:58 train slide shut in front of her face. Three times, she slipped into class after the bell, cheeks burning.

"You are not slow," her grandmother said one evening over dinner. "Your mornings are just crowded. You are trying to fit forty minutes of jobs into twenty minutes of time."

That night, Grandma helped Mina make a plan. First, they wrote down every job Mina did in the morning and how long each one really took. Getting dressed: 10 minutes. Breakfast: 15 minutes. Washing up and brushing teeth: 10 minutes. Finding things: anywhere from 2 to 15 minutes! "There is your thief," Grandma said, tapping the last line. "The time isn't disappearing. It is being stolen by a missing sock."

So they moved some jobs to the night before. Mina packed her bag after finishing her homework, laid her uniform on the chair, and set her shoes by the door. She put her alarm clock across the room, so she had to get out of bed to switch it off. Finally, she wrote her new routine on a sticky note and stuck it to the bathroom mirror: 6:50 up, 7:00 wash, 7:10 dress, 7:20 breakfast, 7:40 shoes, 7:45 GO.

The first morning was hard. The alarm buzzed across the room, and Mina groaned, stumbled over, and turned it off. But since she was already standing, she kept going. Her bag was waiting. Her sock was where she had left it. At 7:43, she was tying her shoes. At 7:52, she was standing on the platform with six minutes to spare, watching the arrival screen count down: 3 minutes, 2 minutes, 1 minute.

By the end of the month, Mina had not been late once. Her commute felt calmer, too. Instead of running, she had time to read a few pages of her book on the train. Her teacher noticed and called her the most punctual student in the class, which made Mina laugh, because a month earlier she had been the least.

One Sunday, Mina's little cousin complained that he was always late for his taekwondo class. Mina grabbed a sticky note and a pencil. "First," she said, "let's find your thief."
