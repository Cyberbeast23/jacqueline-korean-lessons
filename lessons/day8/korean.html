🇰🇷 Korean Day 8: Daily Routines & Telling Time

Welcome back, Jacqueline! This week we follow a day in the life in Seoul: 하루 일과 (haru ilgwa, daily routine) and telling time. You'll learn 10 new words with Hangul, Revised Romanization, English meaning, and listen-and-say practice for every word. By the end you can say when you get up and go to bed, name the three meals of the day, ask 몇 시예요? (what time is it?), and tell someone you're in a hurry. Example sentences reuse earlier words (밥, 가족, 공부해요, 김밥) so you keep them fresh.

How to read Revised Romanization:
  • Letters approximate the sound in English. Korean does not use tone numbers like Cantonese.
  • Example: jeonyeok — roughly juh-nyuhk. eo is an open "uh" sound (like the u in cup), and the final k is barely released: your tongue just stops.
  • In si (시) and sigan (시간), the ㅅ before i sounds like "shee", so 시 is said almost like she. 시간 ≈ shee-gan.
  • Sound change: a final consonant slides into a following vowel. 아침 + 을 = achimeul; 일어나요 itself is 일 + 어 = i-reo-na-yo (the l turns into a soft r before a vowel); 몇 시 is said myeot ssi (the s gets a little stronger).
  • Two number systems: hours use native Korean numbers (한 시, 두 시, 세 시, 네 시, 다섯 시…), but minutes use Sino-Korean numbers (오 분, 십 분, 삼십 분). 7:30 = 일곱 시 삼십 분 (ilgop si samsip bun), or simply 일곱 시 반 (half past seven).

Culture tip: A school day in Seoul can be long! Many students eat a hot 점심 (lunch) together in the school cafeteria, then after school go to a 학원 (hagwon), an after-school academy for math, English, piano, or taekwondo, and may not get home until 저녁. Seoul's subway is famous for being on time, and the screens show exactly how many 분 until the next train. When Koreans greet each other at mealtimes they often ask 밥 먹었어요? (bap meogeosseoyo?): "Have you eaten?" It is a friendly way of saying "How are you?"
