A Day on the Seoul Subway

At 8:30 on a Saturday morning, Leah stood at the top of the stairs at Hongik University Station and took a deep breath. Three days earlier she had flown from Vancouver to Seoul with her family, and today, for the first time, she was going to ride the subway on her own. Her cousin Jiwoo would be waiting at Gyeongbokgung Palace at ten o'clock. On a sticky note, Leah's mom had written the route: green Line 2 to Euljiro 3-ga, transfer to orange Line 3, get off at Gyeongbokgung, Exit 5.

At the gate, Leah tapped her T-money card on the reader. It beeped and flashed her balance, and the barrier swung open. Seoul's subway carries millions of passengers every day, yet it is famously efficient: at rush hour a train can arrive every few minutes, and the lines are numbered and colour-coded so that even a newcomer can follow them. Every station also has a number, and the signs are written in Korean and English. Leah found that comforting. She could not read every Hangeul word yet, but she could follow a green arrow.

On the platform, people waited in tidy lines on painted marks beside the glass safety doors. When the train pulled in, nobody pushed; everyone let the passengers inside step off first. The car was surprisingly quiet. Most riders were looking at their phones, and the few who spoke kept their voices low. Leah was about to sit down in an empty pink seat when she noticed the picture above it: a pregnant woman. It was a priority seat. She stood and held the strap instead, quietly pleased that she had remembered the subway etiquette her aunt had described.

The trouble began at Euljiro 3-ga. Leah followed the orange signs, hurried down a staircase, and jumped onto a Line 3 train just as the doors closed. Two minutes later, a calm voice announced, "This stop is Chungmuro." Leah's stomach dropped. Chungmuro was not on her sticky note. She had boarded a train going in the wrong direction.

She stepped off and looked around. The signs were full of station names she did not recognize. Then she remembered the phrase she had practised on the plane. Turning to a grandmother holding a shopping bag, she asked, "저기요, 경복궁역이 어디예요?" (Excuse me, where is Gyeongbokgung Station?) The woman's face lit up. She pointed across the platform and said slowly, "오른쪽으로 가세요." Leah understood: go to the right. "감사합니다!" she said, and bowed slightly.

Leah reached Exit 5 twenty minutes late, but Jiwoo just laughed. "Everyone takes the wrong direction once," she said. Then she shared the trick that local riders use: each platform sign shows the name of the last station on the line, and that name tells you which way the train is heading. "Check the direction before you check the doors," Jiwoo said.

That evening, riding home, Leah found her own way back without looking at the note even once. She had learned to navigate the city one station at a time. The subway map on the wall no longer looked like a bowl of tangled noodles. It looked like a puzzle she knew how to solve.
