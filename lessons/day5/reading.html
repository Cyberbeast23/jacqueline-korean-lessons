The Extra Apple

Every Saturday, Grandma Kim walked to the traditional market near her apartment in Seoul, and this week Mina, visiting from Toronto, went with her. Mina had imagined a quiet grocery store with neat shelves. Instead, the market was bustling: hundreds of tiny stalls squeezed together under a long glass roof, sellers calling out prices, and shoppers weaving between carts piled with cabbages. Steam rose from a stall where a woman flipped golden mung-bean pancakes, and the smell of sesame oil made Mina's stomach growl.

Grandma Kim had a list, but she did not buy anything right away. First she walked slowly down the whole row of fruit stalls, looking at the apples, pears, and bright orange persimmons. "Look before you buy," she told Mina. "The first price you see is not always the best one." At one stall, apples were ₩2,000 each. Three stalls later, they were five for ₩8,000. Mina did the math in her head and grinned. The second stall was the better bargain.

The vendor at that stall was an older man in a padded vest who seemed to know everyone. "할머니, 오랜만이에요!" (Grandma, it's been a while!) he called. Grandma Kim laughed and introduced her granddaughter. Mina had been practising for this moment. She pointed at the apples and asked carefully, "이거 얼마예요?" (How much is this?)

The man's eyebrows shot up. "Five for eight thousand won," he answered in Korean, slowly, so she could follow. Mina counted out the money with both hands, the way Grandma had shown her, and said, "사과 다섯 개 주세요." (Five apples, please.) The vendor filled a black plastic bag — and then, with a wink, dropped in a sixth apple. "덤이에요," he said. It's a bonus.

On the walk home, Mina asked about the extra apple. Grandma Kim explained that deom is an old market custom. A generous seller adds a little extra for a loyal customer, and the customer keeps coming back. "Supermarkets have lower prices sometimes," she said, "but they don't remember your name." She told Mina that many traditional markets had struggled when big stores and online shopping arrived, and some had closed. The ones that survived often did so because of relationships: sellers who knew which family liked crisp apples and which grandmother needed her rice delivered.

That night, Mina wrote in her journal about the market. She described the steam, the shouting, and the crowded rows, but most of all she wrote about the moment her Korean worked. One small question, 이거 얼마예요?, had turned a stranger into someone who smiled at her. She decided that next Saturday she would go back, pay attention to the prices, and say thank you before Grandma could remind her.

The sixth apple sat on the kitchen table until morning. Mina almost did not want to eat it. It felt less like fruit and more like a welcome.
