Full Moon, Full House

Every autumn, on the fifteenth day of the eighth month of the lunar calendar, the moon over Korea grows round and bright. That full moon marks Chuseok, a harvest festival that is often compared to Thanksgiving. For about three days, offices close, schools pause, and highways fill with cars. Tens of millions of trips are made as people leave big cities like Seoul and head back to the towns where their parents and grandparents live. A drive that normally takes four hours can stretch past eight. Nobody enjoys the traffic, but almost everyone agrees the trip is worth it.

Why travel so far? Because Chuseok is, above all, a family reunion. Many families begin the holiday morning with charye, a ceremony in which they set a table with rice, fruit, fish, and rice cakes and bow to honour their ancestors. Before the holiday, relatives often visit family graves to trim the grass and tidy the site, a custom called beolcho. These actions say something important: a family is not only the people at today's table but also the generations who came before.

Then there is the cooking. The star of the season is songpyeon, small half-moon rice cakes stuffed with sesame and honey, sweet beans, or chestnuts. Families sit in a circle, pinching and folding the dough together. The cakes are steamed on a bed of pine needles, which gives them a faint, fresh scent. An old saying claims that whoever shapes the prettiest songpyeon will one day have a beautiful daughter, so even the youngest cousins try hard to make theirs neat. The kitchen becomes a classroom where grandparents pass down recipes, jokes, and stories.

Language at a Korean family gathering also follows patterns. Younger people rarely call older relatives by their first names. Instead, they use titles that show the relationship: a girl calls her older sister eonni, while a boy calls his older sister nuna. Grandmother is halmeoni, and any younger brother or sister is a dongsaeng. When speaking to elders, people choose polite, honorific forms of verbs and often bow deeply on the holiday morning. Grandparents may answer with words of blessing and, sometimes, a little pocket money for the children.

Chuseok is changing, too. Some families now share smaller meals, skip the long drive, or gather by video call when relatives live overseas. Korean-Canadian families in cities like Vancouver and Toronto may celebrate on the nearest weekend, buying songpyeon from a Korean bakery and cooking a mix of traditional and Canadian dishes. A few families even take holiday trips together instead of hosting at home. The details shift, but the core idea stays the same.

That core idea is gratitude. The harvest reminds people that food does not appear by magic; it comes from seasons of work. The bows remind them that they stand on the shoulders of those before them. And the crowded, noisy kitchen reminds everyone, young and old, that belonging is something you build with your hands, one songpyeon at a time, under a very full moon.
