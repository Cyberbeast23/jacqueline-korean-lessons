A Gift of Letters

Imagine wanting to write a note to your grandmother but having to memorize thousands of complicated characters first. For most of Korea's history, that was reality. Koreans spoke Korean, but official writing used Chinese characters, which took years of study to master. Rich families could afford that time; farmers, merchants, and most women could not. As a result, only a small group of scholars could read laws, letters, or even the price list at a market.

In the 1440s, King Sejong decided that was unfair. He worked with a team of scholars to design something completely new: an alphabet made just for the sounds of Korean. In 1446 he shared it with the public in a book called Hunminjeongeum, which means "the correct sounds for teaching the people." In the introduction, the king explained that he felt sorry for ordinary people who had something to say but no way to write it down. A wise person, he said, could learn the new letters in a single morning, and even a slower learner could manage it in ten days.

What makes Hangeul, as the alphabet is now called, such a remarkable innovation is its design. Many of the consonants are drawn in the shape of the mouth making the sound. The letter ㄱ (g/k) shows the back of the tongue lifting toward the throat, ㄴ (n) shows the tip of the tongue touching the ridge behind the top teeth, and ㅁ (m) is a square for closed lips. The vowels come from three simple symbols: a dot for the sky, a flat line for the earth, and a standing line for a person. Letters are then stacked into neat syllable blocks, so 한 (han) is built from ㅎ, ㅏ, and ㄴ.

Not everyone cheered. Some officials argued that abandoning Chinese characters would cut Korea off from respected learning, and for centuries many writers continued to look down on Hangeul. Yet the alphabet survived in letters, songs, and stories, especially among women and common people. Over time it became a symbol of Korean identity. Today South Korea has one of the highest literacy rates in the world, and the original 1446 book is listed in UNESCO's Memory of the World register.

Every year on October 9, Koreans celebrate Hangeullal, or Hangeul Day. Families may visit exhibits, schools hold handwriting and calligraphy contests, and people post their favourite Korean words online. In Canada, many Korean families mark the day at Saturday Korean-language schools, where children practise writing their names in Hangeul and learn why their alphabet is unusual: it is one of the few writing systems in the world whose inventor, date, and purpose are all clearly recorded.

Nearly six centuries later, King Sejong's idea still feels modern. He believed that reading should not belong only to the powerful. Every time a student in Seoul or Vancouver writes 안녕하세요 in a notebook, that belief comes alive again, one tidy syllable block at a time.
