The Gimjang Backache

Every November, Grandma Kim's apartment turned into a kimchi factory. It was gimjang season, when families gather to make enough kimchi to last the whole winter. This year, Mina was finally old enough to help. Aunties sat on plastic mats salting mountains of napa cabbage, cousins chopped radish, and the kitchen smelled of garlic, ginger, and bright red chili flakes.

Mina wanted to prove she was strong. When Uncle Joon asked for help moving a huge plastic tub of salted cabbage, she rushed over before anyone else could. She bent at the waist, locked her knees, grabbed the handles, and yanked. The tub rose a few centimetres, and then something in her lower back gave a sharp twinge. She set the tub down quickly. "괜찮아요!" she said, smiling. But by dinnertime she could hardly sit up straight.

The next morning, Grandma took her to a small clinic around the corner. The doctor, Dr. Park, was calm and patient. "어디가 아파요?" she asked. Mina pointed to her 허리, just above her hips. Dr. Park pressed gently on the muscles and asked Mina to bend, twist, and lift each leg. "Good news," she said. "Nothing is broken. You have strained a muscle. It is like a rubber band that was stretched too hard, too fast."

Then Dr. Park drew a quick sketch on a notepad. In the first picture, a stick figure bent over a box with straight legs and a curved back. In the second, the figure squatted down, kept the box close to its body, and stood up by pushing through its legs. "Your legs have the biggest muscles in your body," she explained. "Let them do the heavy work, and your back stays safe. Keep the load close, don't twist while you lift, and ask for help when something is too heavy. Asking for help is not weak. It is smart."

Dr. Park didn't give Mina a bed-rest order. Instead, she taught her three gentle stretches and told her to keep walking a little each day, because moving helps a sore muscle recover faster than lying still. She suggested a warm pack in the evenings and said that if the pain got worse instead of better, Mina should come back.

That weekend, the whole family went to a jjimjilbang, a Korean bathhouse with warm, heated floors. Mina lay on a woven mat in her loose cotton outfit, eating a roasted egg, while the warmth soaked into her back. Each day she did her stretches and walked to the corner store with Grandma. By Thursday, the twinge was almost gone.

On the last day of gimjang, Uncle Joon pointed at another full tub. Mina grinned, called her cousin over, and squatted down beside it. Knees bent, back straight, tub close, they lifted together and carried it to the balcony without a wobble. Grandma clapped. "This year," she said, "the kimchi is good, and the kimchi-maker is even smarter."
